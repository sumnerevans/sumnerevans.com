My dad spent a long and successful career as a mining engineer. Shortly after he
married my mom, his mother in law, my Oma, asked what he did. He said "I tell
people to dig over there". I sure hope there were people in heaven who could
actually explain what he did when Oma got there.

I was reminded of this story today, and asked myself the question: what would I
say about my job? The most obvious example would be "I tell Claude to write
code".

But is that really my job?

And that got me thinking: how _do_ I actually explain what I do? Of course, in
casual conversation, a pithy description of my day-to-day is often the correct
response. But what about to my manager, project managers, company leadership, or
potential employers? How should I explain what I do to them?

As a tech lead, I basically have two jobs to describe: software engineer and
engineering manager.

I actually think that engineering management is easier to explain than software
engineering because everyone can understand when you say "enable and grow the
team's capabilities" or "improve or establish processes for the team". Although
I haven't been a manager in any other context, but engineering management seems
to have many similarities to other management positions. Due to the economic
necessity of justifying ones existence within a company org chart despite
performing no tactical work, I think that managers have gotten reasonably good
at describing what they do.

Software engineers, in contrast, have not had to justify their existence until
the last few years. Software engineers were seen as "code sorcerers" who had
this special ability to "understand computer". We described what we do as
"writing code", and we demanded protection of our time so that we could get into
a flow state and focus on "writing code".[^1]

When we said "writing code is hard", and leadership heard "typing code is hard".
But how much of what we described as "writing code" was actually "typing code"?

With the advent of AI, the cost of typing code has been driven to zero and what
has emerged is that there is a big distinction between "writing code" and
"typing code". We as software engineers knew this, but we never had to justify
the difference because "typing code" had such a high barrier to entry that it
made sense that "typing code" is just extremely difficult and time consuming.

With that barrier to entry gone, now anyone can type code quickly. And since we
described so many things as "writing code" (which leadership understands as
"typing code"), the natural conclusion of leadership is that AI can drive the
cost of "writing code" to zero.
